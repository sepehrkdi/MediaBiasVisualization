The Lens Through Which We See

Every day, millions of news articles shape our perception of global events. But what stories get told—and how—varies dramatically depending on who's telling them.

This project analyzes GDELT (Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone), one of the largest open datasets of global news coverage, to expose patterns in how different media outlets report on the same events.

We examine sentiment, coverage volume, geographic focus, and thematic emphasis to reveal the invisible editorial choices that influence public understanding.

The Balkans: Europe's Bloodiest Decade

The breakup of Yugoslavia (1989-2000) produced Europe's deadliest conflicts since World War II. The distribution of violence tells a stark story.

Dominance: Bosnia-Herzegovina alone accounts for an extraordinary 86.4% of all documented events—making it one of the most concentrated conflicts in our dataset.

This concentration meant intense Western media focus, with journalists able to cover the conflict from relatively accessible European locations.

Central Africa: The "Forgotten" Continent

From 2000 to 2024, Central Africa has been home to some of the world's deadliest conflicts—yet they receive a fraction of the media coverage.

The Numbers: DR Congo (Zaire) dominates with 54% of events. The Second Congo War (1998-2003) and ongoing Kivu conflicts have killed millions, yet remain largely invisible to Western audiences.

⚠️ Media Gap: Despite comparable or higher death tolls than better-known conflicts, Central African crises struggle for international attention.

The Middle East: Disproportionate Focus

From 2010 to 2024, the Middle East has dominated global headlines. But look at how coverage aligns—or doesn't—with actual conflict intensity.

The Paradox: Syria accounts for a staggering 80% of regional events. Meanwhile, Yemen—highlighted here—represents only 4.9% of events despite being called the world's worst humanitarian crisis.

South Asia: Pre-9/11 Landscape

Before 2001 transformed global attention on the region, South Asia (1989-2000) had a very different conflict landscape.

Surprising Distribution: India leads with 46% of events (Kashmir, Punjab insurgencies), while Afghanistan—later the focus of a 20-year war—accounts for just 19.5% during this period.

The post-9/11 shift would completely reshape how the world perceived this region.

Southeast Asia: Myanmar's Tragedy

In recent years (2010-2024), Southeast Asia's conflict landscape has been dominated by one country's descent into crisis.

Concentrated Crisis: Myanmar accounts for 66% of regional events. The Rohingya genocide and 2021 military coup have made it one of the world's most pressing humanitarian emergencies.

The 2021 coup briefly captured global attention—but has Myanmar received sustained coverage proportional to its crisis?

The Real Severity: Casualties per Event

Now let's look at the intensity of these conflicts—average casualties per documented event. This reveals a shocking disparity.

The Paradox: Despite receiving less media coverage, Liberia's conflicts were consistently 3-6× deadlier per event than Sierra Leone's. The dashed lines show each country's average over the entire period.

In 1996, Liberia averaged 99 casualties per event—nearly 10× higher than Sierra Leone's 9.4. Yet Sierra Leone dominated the headlines.

⚠️ Why the gap? Media infrastructure, journalist access, geopolitical interests, and narrative simplicity all influence which conflicts receive attention—often independent of actual human cost.

03

Methodology

Understanding how we collected, processed, and visualized this data is essential for interpreting the results critically.

Data Sources

GDELT (Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone) monitors broadcast, print, and web news media from nearly every country. It processes over 100,000 articles daily, extracting events, actors, sentiment, and themes.

  • Events Table: Who did what to whom, when, and where
  • GKG (Global Knowledge Graph): Themes, emotions, and locations
  • Mentions Table: Links events to specific articles

Important: GDELT has English-language bias and relies on automated processing that can introduce errors.

Data Processing Pipeline

Raw GDELT data undergoes several transformations before visualization:

  1. Extraction: PostgreSQL queries retrieve relevant events, filtered by date range and quality thresholds
  2. Cleaning: Remove duplicates, handle missing values, normalize source names
  3. Aggregation: Calculate metrics at source, region, and time period levels
  4. Metric Computation: Derive sentiment scores, coverage indices, and bias indicators
  5. Export: Output JSON/CSV files optimized for web visualization

Bias Metrics

We employ multiple metrics to capture different dimensions of media bias:

  • Sentiment Deviation: How far a source's average tone deviates from the global mean for similar events
  • Coverage Selectivity: Which stories a source chooses to cover vs. ignore
  • Geographic Concentration: How focused coverage is on specific regions
  • Actor Framing: Whether specific actors are consistently portrayed positively or negatively

Limitation: These metrics capture patterns but cannot definitively establish intent or distinguish bias from legitimate editorial focus.

Limitations & Caveats

This analysis has significant limitations that must be acknowledged:

  • Source Bias: GDELT's source collection is not comprehensive; smaller outlets and non-English media are underrepresented
  • Automated Processing: Event extraction and sentiment analysis use algorithms that can misclassify content
  • Temporal Gaps: Coverage may be inconsistent for certain periods or regions
  • Definitional Ambiguity: "Bias" is contested; our metrics operationalize one interpretation
  • Correlation ≠ Causation: Patterns don't reveal why outlets make editorial decisions

Uncertainty & Confidence

We communicate uncertainty throughout the visualizations:

  • Confidence Intervals: Shown on hover for aggregated metrics
  • Sample Size Indicators: Visual encoding of data volume underlying each point
  • Missing Data: Explicitly marked rather than interpolated or hidden
  • Quality Flags: Data quality warnings appear when thresholds aren't met

Technical Implementation

This project uses the following technology stack:

  • Data Storage: PostgreSQL database with GDELT schema
  • Preprocessing: Python (pandas, psycopg2, numpy)
  • Visualization: D3.js v7
  • Frontend: Vanilla HTML, CSS, JavaScript
  • Hosting: GitHub Pages

All code is open source and available in our GitHub repository .

About This Project

Project Context

This visualization project was developed as part of the Data Visualization course at the University of Genova (2025-2026).

Our goal is to make complex media analysis accessible and to encourage critical thinking about news consumption. We believe transparency about methodology and limitations is essential for responsible data journalism.
